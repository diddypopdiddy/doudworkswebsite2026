AI integrity / shared language

Classroom expectations

Build shared language, shared workflows, and an expectation that AI-supported thinking can be explained.

Human check

What is the learning goal? What must the person still decide?

01

Build common language

Teach the class a small, repeatable vocabulary: the AI or no-AI lane, the five AI Modes, and the four AI Core Competencies. Shared language makes expectations easier to plan, discuss, and revisit across assignments.

02

Start with the task

  • Name the learning goal before naming a tool.
  • Identify the thinking, authorship, skill, or relationship that must remain human.
  • Choose no AI or an intentional AI Mode: Idea, Learning, Feedback, Tool, or Practice.
  • State what students should keep, show, submit, or discuss.
03

Use all four competencies every time

  • Prompting: choose the goal, context, sources, constraints, and AI role with care.
  • Data & Privacy: use approved tools, minimize information, and remove identifiable or protected data.
  • Ethics: consider permission, authorship, fairness, access, bias, and possible harm.
  • Verification: identify the check before use, then test important claims and document changes.
04

Conversation is cognition

Expect students to talk about AI-supported work and, when it matters, show the meaningful parts of the AI conversation. Prompts, Mode choices, follow-up questions, rejected output, revisions, and checks are evidence of thinking.

This does not require surveillance or every token. Use a curated chatlog, selected screenshots, a before-and-after artifact, or a short process note that fits the task.

05

Make the process count

Give credit for fitting the AI use to the task, improving prompts, protecting data, considering ethics, verifying claims, revising the work, and explaining human decisions. A polished AI answer by itself is not strong evidence of learning.

06

Show good and poor uses

  • Learning Mode: try first, use AI to question or explain, then demonstrate the idea independently—not copy the answer.
  • Feedback Mode: bring work already begun and decide what to revise—not ask AI to replace the draft.
  • Tool Mode: transform privacy-safe information and check the result—not upload identifiable student data.
  • Verification: choose a reliable check before prompting—not accept confident output because it sounds right.
07

A repeatable classroom routine

  • Before: name the goal, lane, AI Mode, human work, and verification route.
  • During: prompt intentionally, protect data, question the output, and keep meaningful evidence.
  • Turn in: include the final work and the process evidence requested for the task.
  • Talk: explain what AI did, what changed, what was rejected, how claims were checked, and what remains your own decision.
08

Leave room for no AI

A healthy classroom has room for AI and room for no AI. The correct lane depends on the learning goal, privacy, access, student readiness, and whether AI would strengthen or replace the work that matters.
