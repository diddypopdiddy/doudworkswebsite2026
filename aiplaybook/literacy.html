AI Literacy in the Framework

Goal

Establish a shared vocabulary and a shared understanding, so the class can talk about AI without the myths.

Learning objectives

  1. Students will be able to define the key vocabulary in their own words.
  2. Students will be able to explain that a model learns patterns from examples and then makes predictions.
  3. Students will be able to place today’s chat tools in a short history of rules, machine learning, neural networks and generative AI.
  4. Students will be able to name one limit of AI output and explain why it needs checking.

Key vocabulary

AI
Artificial intelligence. A family of systems that can predict, classify, recommend or generate.
Predict
Estimate an outcome or what is likely to come next.
Classify
Assign something to a category.
Recommend
Suggest an option that may fit a person’s needs or interests.
Generate
Make new content such as text, an image or audio.
Machine learning
An approach that learns patterns from examples to make predictions.
Training and training data
Training is when a model learns patterns. Training data are the examples it learns from.
Neural network
Layers of math that can learn complex patterns from data.
Generative AI
AI that creates new content using patterns it learned during training.
Language model
A model that learns patterns in language and can generate text by predicting one piece at a time.
Prompt
The input or instruction you give a model.
Token
A small unit of text, such as a word, part of a word or punctuation.

Lesson flow

Day 1 teaches the vocabulary through a demo and a short history. Day 2 practices it, clears up the myths and ends with the quiz.

Day 1 · Teach it

Opening

  1. 5 min
    Where did you meet AI before first period?
    Teacher
    Collect examples such as recommendations, spam filters, maps, photo search and autocomplete. Ask what goes in and what comes out.
    Students
    Give one example and sort it as predict, classify, recommend or generate. Explain the choice to a partner.
    Say thisA chatbot is one kind of AI. What did this system do with its input?

Teach

  1. 12 min
    Train and test a classifier in Teachable Machine
    Teacher
    Project the two-class demo with objects only: no student faces, no voices, no sign-in. Introduce each vocabulary word when students see its job.
    Students
    Predict the label before each test. Explain what the training examples helped the model do.
    Say thisWe gave it examples and a set of labels. What can it do when none of those labels fit?
    The demo in four moves
    1. Name two labelsPen and Mug
    2. Show examplesThis is the training data
    3. Train the modelThis is machine learning
    4. Test it, then show a spoonIt can only classify as Pen or Mug
    Click-by-click steps
    1. On the teacher device, open Teachable Machine. Choose Image Project and Standard image model. Label the two classes Pen and Mug.
    2. Use the webcam or your own object images to give the model several views of each object. Call these examples training data. Select Train Model and describe this pattern learning as machine learning.
    3. Use Preview to show a new view of a pen or mug. Ask students to say what the model is doing when it picks a label. Introduce classify.
    4. Show the spoon. With only pen and mug as possible labels, any label it gives for the spoon is wrong. Ask what examples or choices could help.
    Before the demo

    Check current terms and school approval. Use only objects on the teacher’s device. Keep student faces and voices out of the camera or mic. Run the demo without signing in, saving to Drive or hosting a model. Google describes an on-device option for training. Google’s Teachable Machine guide · Google’s training and data explanation

    Without a device

    Give pairs a few labeled pen and mug pictures. Have them describe patterns, label two new pictures and then try labeling a spoon using only the two available labels. Ask what is missing from their examples and label choices.

  2. 10 min
    From a classifier to a language model
    Teacher
    Project the picture below. Then write two sentence stems, such as “Snow usually feels ___” and “The coolest weekend plan is ___”, and let the class be the model.
    Students
    Offer likely next pieces of text and compare them. Identify the prompt and a possible token. Say which stem had one likely answer and which had many.
    Say thisThat model predicts a label. A chat tool predicts the next piece of text. Same idea, much bigger.
    Learning · before anyone uses it
    1. Lots of examplesText people have written
    2. Practice predictingGuess the next piece
    3. Adjust the settingsA little closer each time
    4. Repeat, many timesResult: a trained model
    Answering · when you ask
    1. Your promptPlus earlier turns in the chat
    2. Split into tokensSmall pieces of text
    3. Predict the next pieceThe likely one, not the true one
    4. Repeat, piece by pieceResult: a response

Practice

  1. 10 min
    Four steps of history
    Teacher
    Put the four steps along the board. Hand out or read aloud everyday examples, such as a calculator, a spam filter, photo search and a chat tool.
    Students
    Place each example on the timeline and defend the choice. Explain how learning from examples differs from writing fixed rules.
    Say thisHere’s the short version. These ideas overlap, and tools still use more than one approach.
    1. Hand-written rulesPeople write every step.
    2. Machine learningThe system learns patterns from examples.
    3. Neural networksLayers of math learn complex patterns.
    4. Generative AIToday’s chat tools make new text.

Check

  1. 4 min
    Three quick questions
    Teacher
    Cold-call, no notes: What is training data? What does classify mean? What does a language model predict?
    Students
    Answer in your own words and point to the moment in class that showed it.
    Say thisUse your own words. Where did we see that happen today?

Exit

  1. 4 min
    Exit ticket
    Teacher
    Collect one line from everyone. Use it to choose which words to revisit at the start of Day 2.
    Students
    Write one thing that surprised you and what showed it to you.
    Say thisWhat surprised you today? Point to the part of the lesson that helped you see it.

Day 2 · Practice it and check it

Opening

  1. 5 min
    Say it without your notes
    Teacher
    Put four of yesterday’s words on the board. Choose the ones the exit tickets showed were shaky.
    Students
    Define two words to a partner, then give an example of each from the demo.
    Say thisNo notes. Tell your partner what it means and where we saw it.

Teach

  1. 8 min
    Myth or fact?
    Teacher
    Read the six myths one at a time and ask for a reason with each vote. Land on the reality every time.
    Students
    Vote, then point to something from the demo or the picture that supports your call.
    Say thisWhat did we see yesterday that helps you make that call?
    • AI thinks like a person.It detects and generates patterns. Human-sounding language doesn’t prove understanding.
    • AI answers are accurate.Models can make up details, citations and confidence. Important information needs checking.
    • AI always has the live internet.That depends on the product, its settings and what’s connected to it.
    • Using AI is automatically cheating.It depends on what the task allows and whether AI did the thinking being assessed.
    • A detector can prove AI was used.Detector scores are uncertain and can be wrong. Look at the conversation and talk to the student.
    • One perfect prompt solves everything.Good work takes context, follow-up, checking and a person who understands the goal.

Practice

  1. 15 min
    Play the Quiz Board
    Teacher
    Open Workbook Chapter 1 on the projector and run it in 2–4 teams. This is practice, so talk through every miss.
    Students
    Answer as a team and explain one answer in your own words before the next clue.
    Say thisBefore I reveal it, tell us why your team picked that.

Check

  1. 12 min
    Give the suggested quiz
    Teacher
    Hand out the student copy. Allow about 10 minutes and collect it before any discussion.
    Students
    Complete the ten items on your own.
    Say thisUse a concrete example when you explain why you would check an AI response.

Exit

  1. 5 min
    Go over what was missed
    Teacher
    Use the teacher-held answer key to review the two items most students missed. Each item is tagged to an objective, so a miss tells you what to reteach.
    Students
    Correct one answer and write why the right one fits.
    Say thisWhich word would have helped you answer that one?

Check for understanding

Suggested quiz

10 items · About 10 minutes. Use this for ungraded, formative feedback. A quiz result does not issue a Permit.

Printable student copy
Preview the questions

  1. A library tool puts a returned book into one of three topic groups. What is it doing?

    1. Recommending
    2. Classifying
    3. Generating

  2. Which part of a classroom model project is the training data?

    1. The score from its first test
    2. The names of its buttons
    3. The labeled examples it learns from

  3. You type “Compare these two outlines in three bullets.” What is that instruction called?

    1. A prompt
    2. A neural network
    3. A prediction

  4. You counted five words. A language model counted eight tokens. How could both counts be right?

    1. A token has to be a whole sentence
    2. A token only counts when a word repeats
    3. A token can be part of a word or punctuation

  5. One system calculates a total by following a fixed rule. Another creates a new caption from patterns it learned. Which describes the second?

    1. A fixed calculation
    2. Generative AI
    3. A sort into categories

  6. Myth or fact? A model trained only on daytime photos can struggle to recognize the same objects at night.

  7. Myth or fact? If a tool was trained on examples, someone must have written a rule for every possible result.

  8. Myth or fact? A realistic image made by a generative model proves that the pictured event happened.

  9. Put this simplified history in order, earliest to latest. A. Generative AI chat tools. B. Hand-written rules. C. Neural networks. D. Machine learning.

  10. Give one reason to check what AI tells you. Include an example of how you would check it.

Misconceptions and reteach

“I corrected it, so now the model knows.”
Say thisIt can use that correction while answering in this chat. Training changes the model itself.

Redo Have students explain which part of the classifier activity was training and which part was testing.

“It sounded sure, so it must be right.”
Say thisEven a confident label can be wrong. What evidence could we compare it with?

Redo Revisit the unfamiliar object. Have students name what they observed and how they checked the label.

“Rules and machine learning are the same.”
Say thisA rule tells the system what to do. Training examples help a model learn a pattern.

Redo Have one pair sort with a written rule and another sort from examples, then compare.
