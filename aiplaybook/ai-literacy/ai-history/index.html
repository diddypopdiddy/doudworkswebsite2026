AI literacy / scope and sequence

AI history in eight eras

Follow the field from mathematical models of reasoning to generative systems in daily life—without confusing a few famous products for the whole history.

Human check

What is the learning goal? What must the person still decide?

01

Start with the shape

AI history is not a straight march toward smarter machines. It is an overlapping story of symbolic reasoning, statistics, neural networks, search, robotics, human-computer interaction, data, hardware, labor, funding, and public policy.

  • Rules → learned patterns: researchers moved from hand-coding more knowledge toward systems that learn from examples.
  • Narrow programs → reusable models: one system gradually became able to support more kinds of tasks.
  • Research demos → everyday infrastructure: AI moved into search, recommendations, phones, schools, workplaces, and public services.
  • Capability → responsibility: the question expanded from “Can it do this?” to “Should it, for whom, with what evidence, and who is accountable?”
Anchor question: What changed in this era—the idea, the data, the computing power, the interface, or the authority people gave the system?
02

Era 1 · Before 1950 — foundations

Before AI had a name, researchers were building the concepts that made it imaginable: formal logic, probability, computation, information, control, and mathematical models of neurons.

  • 1936: Alan Turing described a general model of computation—an abstract machine that could carry out any computable procedure.
  • 1943: Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts modeled neuron-like units with logic, creating an influential bridge between brains and computation.
  • World War II accelerated digital computing, cryptanalysis, control systems, and institutional investment in computation.
  • What changed: intelligence could be discussed as processes that might be represented and executed, not only as a mysterious human trait.
03

Era 2 · 1950–1956 — a question and a name

The field gained a public question, a research agenda, and a name.

  • 1950: Turing’s “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” proposed the imitation game, shifting attention from defining thought to testing intelligent behavior.
  • 1955: John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon proposed a summer study using the term “artificial intelligence.”
  • 1956: The Dartmouth workshop helped establish AI as an organized field of research.
  • The proposal already named language, abstraction, learning, neural networks, creativity, and self-improvement—questions that remain active today.
  • What changed: AI became a shared research program, not a single invention.
04

Era 3 · 1956–1973 — rules, search, and early demonstrations

Early researchers pursued more than one path. Symbolic AI represented knowledge with logic, rules, and symbols, while connectionist work explored systems that could learn from examples.

  • Symbolic programs solved proofs, played games, searched possible actions, and worked through tightly defined problems.
  • Frank Rosenblatt’s perceptron research offered an early learning-based model rather than a complete set of hand-written rules.
  • 1966: Joseph Weizenbaum’s ELIZA used pattern matching to produce a persuasive conversation without understanding the conversation.
  • Projects such as Shakey combined perception, planning, and physical action in restricted environments.
  • What changed: machines produced striking narrow demonstrations—and revealed how quickly people can mistake fluent behavior for understanding.
05

Era 4 · 1974–1993 — expert systems, neural revival, and AI winters

AI entered repeating cycles of investment and disappointment. Narrow successes did not scale easily to the messy, changing real world.

  • Expert systems such as MYCIN encoded specialist knowledge as rules and showed that AI could perform useful work inside a carefully bounded domain.
  • The first AI winter followed unmet promises and reduced funding in the 1970s; a second downturn followed the expert-system boom in the late 1980s and early 1990s.
  • Expert systems could be brittle, expensive to build, and difficult to update because people had to extract and maintain so much knowledge by hand.
  • 1986: backpropagation research showed a practical way for multilayer neural networks to learn useful internal representations.
  • What changed: the field learned that a convincing demo is not the same as a durable system—and that hype and funding shape which ideas survive.
06

Era 5 · 1994–2009 — statistics, data, and narrow systems

Machine learning increasingly emphasized probability, evaluation, and patterns learned from data rather than trying to hand-code every rule about the world.

  • Speech recognition, computer vision, spam filtering, search, recommendation, and fraud detection became important practical applications.
  • 1997: IBM’s Deep Blue defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov. It was a powerful specialized system, not general intelligence.
  • The web created enormous datasets and real demand for ranking, prediction, personalization, and language technologies.
  • 2009: ImageNet organized millions of labeled images into a large research dataset and benchmark.
  • What changed: datasets, benchmarks, computing infrastructure, and evaluation became as important as the algorithm.
07

Era 6 · 2010–2016 — deep learning changes the scale

Large datasets, graphics processors, improved training methods, and neural networks combined to produce major gains in perception and pattern recognition.

  • 2012: AlexNet dramatically improved large-scale image classification using a deep convolutional neural network trained with GPUs.
  • Deep learning spread through vision, speech recognition, translation, and other systems that could learn useful representations from data.
  • 2016: AlphaGo combined deep neural networks, reinforcement learning, and search to master Go at a level long considered out of reach.
  • What changed: systems learned richer features from large datasets instead of relying only on features designed by people.
08

Era 7 · 2017–2021 — transformers and foundation models

A new architecture made it easier to train very large models across language and, later, other kinds of media.

  • 2017: “Attention Is All You Need” introduced the Transformer, using self-attention and parallel training instead of processing a sequence only one step at a time.
  • Pretraining on broad datasets followed by adaptation allowed one model to support many downstream tasks.
  • 2020: GPT-3 showed that a large language model could attempt new tasks from written instructions and examples without task-specific retraining.
  • The label “foundation model” captured the growing idea that one broadly trained model could become the base for many products and uses.
  • What changed: scale, pretraining, and prompting became central parts of the interface between people and models.
09

Era 8 · 2022–present — generative AI becomes everyday infrastructure

Generative AI moved from specialist research and products into widely available conversational tools. The models were important, but the accessible interface and rapid distribution changed public life.

  • November 2022: ChatGPT’s conversational release made large language models directly usable by a mass audience.
  • Generative systems expanded across text, images, audio, video, code, multimodal input, tool use, and multi-step workflows.
  • The central questions now include verification, privacy, bias, authorship, labor, access, energy, copyright, safety, and human accountability.
  • UNESCO’s 2021 ethics recommendation, the 2023 NIST AI Risk Management Framework, and the European Union’s 2024 AI Act reflect the shift from voluntary experimentation toward governance.
  • What changed: AI became a social and institutional system—not only a model in a lab.
10

The recurring pattern

  • A bold claim creates attention.
  • A compelling demonstration attracts funding and adoption.
  • Wider use reveals limits that a controlled demonstration did not show.
  • Researchers revise the approach, combine older ideas, or wait for better data and computing.
  • A later generation often rediscovers an older idea under new technical conditions.
11

Use this as a classroom sequence

  • First pass: name the eight eras and the dominant approach in each.
  • Second pass: identify what enabled the change—ideas, data, compute, interface, labor, funding, or policy.
  • Third pass: compare the public promise with the actual capability and limitation.
  • Final pass: ask what the milestone changed for people, institutions, and responsibility—not only for benchmark performance.
Dates are anchors, not hard borders. Research traditions overlap, and no single company, country, model, or inventor owns the whole story.
